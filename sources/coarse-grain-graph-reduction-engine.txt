A reduction model based on parallel reduction of
annotated coarse-grain subexpressions has been developed. In this model a kind of string-reduction is
proposed on the level of parallel tasks. A special
reduction strategy avoids the duplication of work
normally incurred by string reduction. Within parallel tasks pure graph-reduction is performed. An experimental parallel machine has been constructed.
Based on a technique of hybrid simulation speed-up
figures of medium-size application programs have
been measured on the experimental machine.

Functional languages are considered to be well
suited for implementation on parallel computer
architectures. A functional program essentially
consists of one expression, which is recursively
constructed from sub-expressions by means of
function application. A functional program is
evaluated by a process called reduction, where
sub-expressions are reduced to their values in a
certain order. The order of reduction is determined by the reduction strategy. The absence of
side-effects in a functional program guarantees the
independence of sub-expressions. Thus in principle all sub-expressions can be evaluated in parallel. However, in practice only those expressions
are computed in parallel from which the result is
known to be needed in the final answer of the
program.

The most efficient way to implement functional
languages is via the means of graph-reduction [2,3].
In graph-reduction, sub-expressions may be shared
and there m a y even exist cycles. Due to this
sharing computation can be saved if the result of
an expression is needed more than once. However,
within the context of parallel reduction a graphical representation of a functional program may
cause difficulties, when sub-expressions have to be
distributed over a number of different processors.
Our proposal for parallel reduction [8,18] is
based on a conceptual separation of reduction and
the control of parallelism. This implies that the
parallel reduction machine consists of two layers.
In one layer a number of concurrent sequential
reducers are active and in the other layer the
distribution of parallel tasks (sub-expressions)
takes place. A consequence of this separation is
that parallel tasks have to contain a sufficient
amount of computation to outweigh the cost of
sending them to a possibly remote sequential reducer. To identify these coarse grain sub-expressions, the application program must contain annotations, inserted by the programmer. Section 2 will
discuss the architecture and the experimental environment that we have used. In Section 3 it is
shown how application programs have to be transformed and annotated in order to run on the
architecture. Section 4 discusses the kind of measurements we have made and the performance
model we used. The last section presents the results of a number of application programs in
terms of speed-up on our parallel machine compared to sequential execution.

The second network has a star topology and

the internal data-communication of the parallel

been specially geared to produce on-line measurement-data about the reduction of sub-expressions

processors, we have designed a reduction model
based on the concept of a job. A job is an independent and needed sub-expression which contains a sufficient amount of computation to justify
the communication cost involved in the transmission of the job to a remote processor. When a job
is elected for parallel execution, a complete copy
of the graph-structure representing the job is
transmitted. This means that on the level of jobs
(distribution layer) our reduction model is based
on a kind of string-reduction, whereas inside jobs
(reduction layer) real graph-reduction is performed.
The major disadvantage of pure string-reduction is the fact that copying of expressions may
also cause duplication of work. In our case this
would happen if a sub-expression of a job happens to be shared by another job (see Fig. 3).
The shared sub-graph would be evaluated twice,
once as part of the copy of job 1 and once as part
of the copy of job 2. To avoid this disadvantage
we developed a special reduction strategy (the
sandwich strategy) which guarantees that shared
sub-expressions in a job will be evaluated before
they are copied. As an example consider in Fig. 4
the simplified skeleton of a functional program in
SASL [16] that calculates the fast Fourier transform of a list of complex numbers. The "basic

In this partial implementation only one reducer

Furthermore, the load-balancing process has not

calculated a posteriori on the host computer, based
on performance measurements of the partial
implementation. These measurements comprise the
number of graph-nodes that have been transported for each job and the number of reduction
steps performed by each job. Also the actual
transmission times are measured.
Reduction time has not been measured, because the reducer we used was not representative
in this respect. It is based on the interpretation of
a fixed set of combinators and has in recent years
been surpassed in speed by efficient compilers.
Instead we measured the number of reduction
steps and used these measurements to predict the
run-time that would have been achieved if an
efficient compiler had been used. The prediction is
based on a figure of 5000 graph-reduction steps
per second, which is reported in [4] for a state-ofthe-art compiler on a VAX-750. Comparison has
shown that the processors in our machine have

each about the same performance as a VAX-750
for CPU-intensive jobs like graph-reduction.
In literature much higher performance figures
than 5000 reduction steps per second are reported
[6,11]. However, these figures have been obtained
by using small bench-mark programs like the
ubiquitous nfib-number. It is easy for a compiler
to derive strictness information from such a toyprogram and subsequently generate conventional
stack-based machine code. This implies that no
graph-reduction is performed at all and the generated code is essentially the same as that produced
by for instance a C-compiler. In larger programs,
like we have used, it is not easy to derive strictness
information and much graph-reduction will have
to be performed. The best performance obtained
for pure graph-reduction is the quoted figure of
5000 reduction steps per second on a VAX-750.

5. Results

A number of application programs have been
written in SASL, ranging from the fast Fourier
transform to a tidal model of the North Sea [17].
All programs had to be transformed to achieve
control of the grain-size of parallel jobs, as is
explained in Chapter 3.
In Fig. 6 the result of the fast Fourier transform is illustrated. In the vertical direction speedup is plotted against a range of threshold values
on the horizontal axis. The speed-up is calculated
with respect to the untransformed sequential version of the program. The performance loss caused
by the transformation is reflected in the fact that
the leftmost point in figure 6 is slightly below one.

The threshold value in this point (512) does not
permit any parallel jobs to be spawned. The fast
Fourier transform has been applied to a list of 512
complex numbers. Several speed-up curves have
been drawn, each for a different number of available processors. All curves represent optimal schedules of the program on our machine architecture.
Similar illustrations (Figs. 7 and 8) are shown for
the quick-sort algorithm and for a parallel version
of the branch-and-bound algorithm that we used
to calculate the optimum schedules. The threshold
value for quick-sort is again the length of the list,
but the schedule program uses the depth of the
search tree to control the grain size. The input of
quicksort is a list of 1024 values obtained by
applying the sine function to the numbers 1 to
1024. The schedule program is given a list containing the execution profile of seven hypothetical
jobs.

6. Comparison With Other Parallel Reduction Architectures
Our parallel reduction architecture differs from
related proposals and projects in four major
aspects:
Data communication is based on the use of
dual ported memories. The two high-speed parallel buses on memory modules realise the highest
possible data-communication bandwidth between
neighbouring processors. The only proposal that
has some similarity to ours is the Bath concurrent
Lisp machine [13], where processors are interconnected by dual ported memories. These memories,
however, do not contain the heap storage but
merely serve as communication buffers.
Experience shows that in real systems datacommunication represents a major bottle-neck.
Most recent and current proposals do not pay
enough attention to the implementation aspects of
datacommunication. This is due to the fact that
architectures are often simulated. If reasonably
sized application programmes have to be executed
on a machine, the simulation of data-communication behaviour appears to be practically impossible, because of the amount of computation
involved.
- The architecture is based on partly overlapping
local storage spaces provided by the dual ported
memories. Both the use of shared buses and dual
ported memories enable important optimisations
in the transportation of jobs. When a reducer
submits a job for parallel execution to one of the
neighbouring processors, no data communication
is needed when the job resides in the overlapping
storage space.
Most other proposals either use completely
shared memories and accept this bottle-neck [15]
or merely use a collection of processing elements
with local memories, connected by some serial
d a t a c o m m u n i c a t i o n network [20,10,12,5]. All
transputer-based proposals fall in this category
H4].

- T h e architecture does not support a global
address space. This implies that if a job lies outside the local address space of the reducer to
which it has been allocated, it has to be copied to
this local address space. In case an expression in
the functional program is shared by a number of
jobs, the copying of this expression can be done
without the duplication of work. Many proposals
that are based on a local memory architecture still
do support a global address space [20,10,12]. In
our opinion this introduces a considerable amount
of overhead that is difficult to control.
In our proposal the programmer is obliged to
annotate the program to indicate coarse-grain subexpressions. Sometimes it is also necessary to
transform the application program to control the
grainsize of parallel jobs. In Keller's work [12] the
grain-size of parallel computations is assumed to
be the same as a user-defined function. However,
not every user-defined function will have a sufficiently coarse grain-size for a local-memory architecture like we use. H u d a k claims that the serial
combinator forms the ideal grain-size for parallel
computations [10]. However, in our opinion only
some recursive combinators will have the right
grain-size for a coarse-grain architecture. The detection of these combinators is an undecidable
problem and we do not think heuristic methods
will be able to uncover coarse-grain sub-expressions in large application programs.
- The load-balancing decisions are made by a
logically centralised conductor. The conductor
possesses global knowledge of the resource usage
in the system. The load balancing algorithm will
take advantage of this knowledge to achieve a
near optimal distribution of jobs. It will also exploit the existence of the partly overlapped storage
spaces by favouring those allocations that do not
require data-communication. According to our
knowledge all of the recent and current proposals
for parallel reduction machines use some form of
diffusion scheduling [12,5,14]. Distribution decisions are made, based on locally available knowledge about the load of adjacent processors.

7. Conclusion
For a certain class of application programs the
proposed reduction model results in a significant
speedup compared to sequential reduction. A

number of medium-sized application programs
have been developed and via a technique of hybrid simulation the speed-up of these programs on
our machine architecture has been measured.
The reduction model can be efficiently implemented on a coarse grain parallel architecture.
Transformation and annotation of functional
programs are necessary to run them on our parallel machine. The proposed reduction model limits
the class of application programs that can profit
from parallel execution to those programs where
the programmer can explicitly indicate coarsegrain subexpressions. Amongst these programs are
divide-and-conquer algorithms and synchronously
communicating sequential processes.
In programs where parallel sub-expressions are
generated recursively, a threshold mechanism has
to control the grain-size of these expressions. In
our proposal the programmer is responsible for
applying a suitable transformation to the program
in order to achieve the desired behaviour. Future
research is required to develop a general programming methodology to write application programs
that meet the constraints imposed by our parallel
reduction model.
