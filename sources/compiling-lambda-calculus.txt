Preface
Lambda calculus is an abstract term rewriting system that was
originally designed to solve mathematical problems on a sheet of
paper. Paper is an inherently immutable tool, though: each term is
rewritten by placing a modified term under the original one.
Computer systems, on the other hand, are inherently mutable
systems: problems are solved by changing the values of registers.
This book describes how to adapt lambda calculus to computerbased environments, by inter preting it or translating it to a notation
that is better fitted for processing by a register-based machine.
Chapter one of the book describes lambda calculus in general: its
syntax, composition of formulae, common abbreviations, and the
rules that are used to reduce (i.e. compute) lambda terms. This
chapter introduces the basic theory of lambda calculus.
The second chapter illustrates how lambda calculus works in
practice, by applying it to fundamental problems like the
implementation of natural numbers, ordered pairs, and lists. It also
introduces fixed points and recursive formulae.
The third chapter implements a ver y simple subset of the Scheme
language, which is loosely based on lambda calculus.
It starts with a simple interpreter for a tiny subset language and
then modifies it subsequently in order to cover a wider range of
the Scheme language and finally shifts the focus from
inter pretation to compilation and code generation. The full code for
all interpreters and compilers is included in the text.
The concluding chapter presents a language that resembles
lambda calculus more closely, by implementing currying and
par tial evaluation. The implications of the design are outlined and
compared to typical LISP and Scheme implementations. Again, a
working compiler for the language is included in the text.
This book is intended for compiler writers and people who are
interested in lambda calculus and LISPy languages. It focuses on
theor y as well as interpretation and code generation.

Lambda Calculus

Lambda Calculus
Lambda calculus (or λ -calculus) forms the theoretical basis for
most functional programming languages. It is often associated
with concepts like ‘‘anonymous functions’’ or ‘‘closures’’ but we will
star t from the beginning here and have a closer look at the formal
system that is λ -calculus.
A formal system is a mathematical concept for manipulating terms
of a formal language. It is is comprised of three parts:
• notation (syntax)
• theor y (axioms and rules)
• semantics (meaning of terms)
In the following, the notation and theory of λ -calculus will be given
formally and semantics will be specified informally as required.

Notation
The alphabet of λ -calculus (LC) is the following:

λ ( ) a i . . . z i Ai . . . Z i
where a . . . z denotes the letters from a to z , ai denotes the letters
a0 , a1 , . . ., etc. Any letter of the form x 0 is normally written x .
There is an infinite number of letters. Some texts use a, a, etc
instead of a1 , a2 , . . ..
By convention, lower case letters ( x , y , ...) represent variables and
upper case letters ( M , N , ...) represent terms, which may in turn
be variables, abstractions, or applications (see below).
A well-formed formula (wff) is a any combination of symbols from
the alphabet of a formal system that forms a correct term under
the rules of that system. The wffs of LC are defined inductively as
follows [Church1941]:

(W2) If x is a variable and M is a wff, (λ xM) is a wff. (abstraction)
(W3) if M and N are wffs, (MN ) is a wff.

Only combinations of symbols that can be built from the above
rules are valid terms of the LC. For instance:

A variable is similar to a variable in a programming language, with
one minor difference: a variable does not necessarily have a
value, but it can stand for itself. In other words: LC does not assign
any special meaning to a variable other than its lexical form.
Variables may be acted upon by the rules outlined later in this
chapter.
Abstraction can be thought of as the creation of a new function
(λ xM), where x is the independent variable of the function and M
is its term. In pure λ -calculus, x must appear in M , while in λ K calculus (pg 16), it does not have to. This text deals with λ K calculus exclusively.

(App)

((λ x(λ y(yx))) A) = (λ y(yx))[x: =A] = (λ y(yA))
The F in (FN ) is called the function and N is called the argument
in the context of application.
If M in (MN ) is not an abstraction, then (MN ) is in its ‘‘normal
form’’ (pg 14) and the term is not rewritten in any way.
Unfor tunately, application is a bit more complex than outlined here.
We will return to it shor tly (pg 11).

Free and Bound Variables
Given the term ( λ x λ y. xy)y , the result of a naive substitution as
described previously (pg 8) would result in the term λ y. yy , which
is clearly different from λ y. xy . The problem in this case is that the
variable y was captured during substitution, i.e. it was substituted
in a term in which it was already ‘‘bound’’.
A variable x is bound in a term M , if M contains at least one subterm of the form λ xN . A sub-term can be:

Substitution
Using the concept of free and bound variables, more precise rules
for the substitution M[x: =N ] can now be defined [Church1941],
see also [Hankin2004] for a more compact presentation:

(S1)

x[x: = N ] ≡ N

(S2)

y[x: = N ] ≡ y

(S3)

(λ xM)[x: =N ] ≡ λ xM

(S4)

(λ xM)[y: =N ] ≡ λ x(M[y: =N ]),
if x /∈FV (N ) or y /∈FV (M)

(S5)

(λ xM)[y: =N ] ≡ λ z(M[x: =z])[y: =N ],
if x ∈FV (N ), y ∈FV (M), z not in λ xM

(S6)

(MN )[x: =P] ≡ (M[x: =P])(N [x: =P])

The notation M[x: =N ] was introduced by Barendregt, while
Church used the ‘‘substitution’’ operator S Nx M . The notation
M[x/N ] is also used, but less common.
Rule (S1) basically says that substituting a term N for a (free)
variable x results in the term N .
Rule (S2) says that variables not subject to a specific substitution
will not be affected by that substitution, e.g. substituting x will not
change any occurrences of y .
Rule (S3) states that bound variables are unaffected by
substitution, e.g. ( λ xx)[x: =N ] ≡ λ xx .

12

Substitution

Rule (S4) defines naive substitution as described earlier (pg 8).
This rules covers the case where no name capturing takes place
during substitution. (Note: the y /∈FV (M) par t covers the trivial
case where no substitution would take place at all.)
Rule (S5) deals with name capturing. It basically says that when a
name bound in a term M would be captured during substitution,
that name will be replaced with a different name ( z ) first, where z
does not appear in M (neither bound nor free). For example:

(λ x λ y. xy)[x: =y] is converted to (λ x λ z. xz)[x: =y]
Rule (S6), finally, covers substitution in applications by inductively
applying the rules (S1) through (S6) to both the function M and
argument N in (MN ).

Conversion and Reduction
Conversion is the process of rewriting a term according to some
specific rules. In this section, the rules of λ -conversion will be
discussed, i.e. the rules for rewriting λ -terms.
Substitution rule (S5) contains the following conversion:

(Alpha)

λ xM →α λ y(M[x: =y])

if y /∈FV (M) and y /∈BV (M)
This conversion is called α -conversion and it indicates that the
names of bound variables can be substituted in λ -terms, as long
as the new name is not already contained in the term. E.g.: λ xx is
the same as λ yy or λ aa. We say that these terms are ‘‘equal
modulo α -conversion’’ and write:

λ xx ≡a λ yy ≡a λ aa
Note that

λ xy /≡a λ yy
because y ∈FV ( λ xy).
In the remainder of this text, syntactic equality will always be
considered modulo α -conversion, so M ≡ N will always denote
M ≡α N .

Redexes and Normal Forms
A term of the form ( λ xM)N is called a β -redex, i.e. a ‘‘ β -reducible
expression’’. (The words ‘‘term’’ and ‘‘expression’’ are used as
synonyms here.) A term that does not contain any β -redexes is
said to be in β -normal form.
In the following ‘‘redex’’ shall indicate a β -redex and ‘‘normal form’’
(or NF) shall indicate a ‘‘ β -normal form’’.
A term M is said to ‘‘have a normal form’’, if M = N and N is in
normal form.
If a term has a NF, then that NF is unique. Two terms M and N
may be considered to be equal (modulo β -reduction), if they

Reduction Strategies
There are different ways to reduce a λ -term to its NF (if such a
form exists). The most common approaches are normal order
reduction and applicative order reduction.
In normal order reduction, the leftmost redex of a term is always
reduced first, e.g. (the next redex to reduce prints in boldface):

(λ xyz. xz(yz)) ((λ xx) (λ xy. x))
→ (λ yz. ((λ xx) (λ xy. x))z (yz))
→ (λ yz. (λ xy. x)z (yz))
→ (λ yz. (λ y. z) (yz))
→ (λ yz. z)
In applicative order reduction, the ‘‘innermost’’ redexes are always
reduced first. An innermost redex is a redex that does not contain
any other redexes. When there are multiple innermost redexes,
any order can be chosen to evaluate them. (Evaluation is used as
a synonym for reduction in this book.)
In particular, this means that the N in (FN ) is always evaluated
first. In programming jargon this means that functions are ‘‘called
by value’’.
Reduction of the above examples using applicative order looks like
this:

(λ xyz. xz(yz)) ((λ xx) (λ xy. x))
→ (λ xyz. xz(yz)) (λ xy. x)
→ (λ yz. (λ xy. x)z (yz))

Confluence
Each λ -term has only one NF regardless of the reduction strategy
used. This is stated by the Church-Rosser theorem. For a proof,
see [Barendregt1985] or [Hankin2004].
Note that this statement does not say that each reduction strategy
will terminate and produce a NF. It only says that if it does, it will
yield the same NF as all other (terminating) strategies.
An abstract term rewriting system (like λ -calculus) that has the
above proper ty is said to ‘‘have the Church-Rosser (CR) property’’
or ‘‘to be CR’’.
It is formally stated as follows:

above statement holds for all terms that have a normal form under

Extensionality
Two functions N and M are called extensionally equal (or ‘‘in
extension’’), when they reduce to the same values when applied to
the same arguments. Formally:

(Ext)

Mx = Nx
, if x /∈FV (M), x /∈FV (N )
M=N

That is, extensional equality is a notion of equality based upon
behavior. In computer science terms, this would mean that two
programs are equal, if they produce the same results for the same
input.
The notion of intensional equality is not formally definable. It says
that two functions are equal, if they are extensionally equal and
the rules of correspondence implemented by the functions have
the same meaning.
In a computer science context, we may be tempted to say that
functions are in intension if they ‘‘use the same algorithm’’. E.g., all
stable sorting functions would be extensionally equal, but not
intensionally equal.
One consequence of (Ext) is the η rule:

(η )

λ x. Mx = M, if x /∈FV (M)

In fact (Ext) and (η ) are equivalent, see [Barendregt1985] for a
proof.
The λ -calculus extended with (η ) is called λη -calculus or
λ βη -calculus. When also extended with constancy functions, it
may be called λ Kη - or λ Kβη -calculus.
In this text the term ‘‘ λ -calculus’’ is is used in the sense of
λ Kβη -calculus.
Note that, while λ x. Mx is extensionally equal to M , there is still
one difference: the former is in head normal form while the latter
may not be in NF. In par ticular, the latter may not even have a NF
at all. For instance, given Ω ≡ (( λ x. xx)( λ x. xx)),

Variable Convention
The variable convention is a concept that simplifies the
substitution rules (S4) and (S5) significantly. It is introduced in
[Barendregt1985].
Because, due to (Alpha), terms are syntactically equal, even if the
names of their bound variables differ, Barendregt suggested to
treat all λ -terms as representatives of their equivalence classes
modulo α -conversion, e.g.:

λ xx ∈ { λ aa,
λ bb,
λ cc,

... }
An ‘‘equivalence class modulo a relation R’’ is the subset U of a
set S , such that aRb holds for all a, b ∈U . In the above case, this
would be the subset of all λ -terms M, N ∈Λ, such that M ≡α N ,
where Λ is the set of all λ -terms.
This approach allows to choose any member of the equivalence
class for each redex of a term in such a way that no name
collisions between free and bound variables occur.
Using the variable convention, substitution rules (S4) and (S5) can
be simplified to:

(S4a)

(λ xM)[y: =N ] ≡ λ x(M[y: =N ])

Church Numerals
There are multiple ways to define natural numbers in terms of
functions. In [Church1941], the following approach was defined,
which is now widely known as Church numerals or Church
numbers.
Informally, a Church number (CN) n is the n-fold application of a
function f to an argument x . Formally: (Note that we allow the
digits 0, . . . , 9 to appear as variables from this point on.)

0 ≡ λ fx. x
1 ≡ λ fx. fx
2 ≡ λ fx. f ( fx)
3 ≡ λ fx. f ( f ( fx))
...
n ≡ λ fx. f n x
where f n x denotes f applied n times to x . Note that 0 cannot be
defined in this way in pure λ β -calculus, because it is a constancy
function (pg 16).
Church numbers are defined inductively using the number zero
and the successor function S :
(CN)

A Church number is either 0 ≡ λ fx. x or S(m), where
S ≡ λ nfx. f (nfx) and m is a Church number.

Propositional Functions
A propositional function is a function returning a truth value, i.e.
‘‘true’’ or ‘‘false’’. In λ -calculus, truth values are represented by the
combinators T and F :

T ≡ λ xy. x
F ≡ λ xy. y
A combinator is a λ -abstraction without any free variables, i.e.
λ xM is a combinator, if FV ( λ xM) = ∅.
The truth values can be thought of as functions of two values,
where T ignores its second argument and returns its first one, and
F ignores its first argument and returns the second one.

26

Moses Schönfinkel and Haskell Curry both showed that multipleargument functions can always be transformed into higher-order
single-argument functions, i.e. functions returning functions. The
transformation is often referred to as ‘‘curr ying’’ or, less often, as
‘‘schönfinkelization’’. All λ -abstractions of multiple arguments are
functions in curried form:

Tab ≡ (λ xy. x)ab ≡ (λ x. λ y. x)ab → (λ y. a)b → a
The T function consumes the first argument, a, and returns the
abstraction λ y. a, which then consumes the second argument, b,
and returns a.

the disjunction (‘‘or’’), and neg the negation (‘‘not’’):

Fixed Points and Recursion
A fixed point of a function F is a value x such that Fx = x .
For instance, the (only) fixed point of the ‘‘square’’ function y = x 2
would be x = 1, because 12 = 1.
The fixed point theorem says:
‘‘For each function F there exists an x such that Fx = x .’’
Or, more informally: every function has at least one fixed point.
This can be demonstrated in λ -calculus as follows:

(FP)

Given ω ≡ λ x. F(xx) and X ≡ ωω ,
X ≡ (λ x. F(xx))ω = F(ωω ) ≡ FX

More interestingly, rule (FP) allows to construct fixed points for all
kinds of functions.
For example, the fixed point of the identity function I ≡ λ xx would
be:

(λ x. I (xx)) (λ x. I (xx)) ≡ (λ x. (λ xx)(xx)) (λ x. (λ xx)(xx))
→
→ (λ x. xx) (λ x. xx)
≡ Ω
where Ω is the quintessential term without normal form.
Curr y’s fixed point combinator (also called the ‘‘Y combinator’’ or
the ‘‘paradoxical operator’’) is a generalization of the above
principle. It can be used to create the fixed point of any function f :

Y ≡ λ f . (λ x. f (xx)) (λ x. f (xx))
A fixed point combinator Y is a combinator such that for each F ,

YF = F(YF)
That is, YF is a fixed point of F . A proof can be found in
[Barendregt1985].
The Y combinator is particularly interesting, because it allows to
define recursive λ -terms.

Schematic Definitions
In [Church1941] there are two types of definitions: syntactic
equality, like I ≡ λ xx , and schematic definitions, like the following
(the arrow indicates a ‘‘defined as’’ relation here, not a reduction):

[M + N ] → ( plus M N )
[M × N ] → (times M N )
[M N ] → (NM)
A schematic formula, like [M + N ] is converted to the
corresponding λ -term by substituting the variables in the term by
the values that appear in the schema in the places of the same
variables. E.g.:

[5 + 7] = ( plus 5 7)
or

[3 × [5 + 7]] = (times 3 ( plus 5 7))
The = sign in this case denotes a rather informal equality, and a
more rigorous definition of schematic definitions and equality will
not be given here. For the simple examples presented here,
intuition should suffice.
BTW: The interested reader may note the relationship between
schematic definitions and the M-expression notation used in some
LISP textbooks, like [Allen1980].
In programming jargon, schematic definitions are also known as
syntactic sugaring, because they make otherwise obtuse formulae
more palatable to the reader.

Lists
A list can be defined in terms of ordered pairs as follows: A list L
is either equal to ε or to an ordered pair whose second coordinate
is a list. Formally:
(List)

L = ε or L = (x, L) for any list L and term x .

This definition is similar to the LISP-style list: an ordered pair is
comparable to a ‘‘cons cell’’ and ε denotes the end of a list (‘‘nil’’).
However, this approach makes it impossible to test for the end of
list because, in pure λ -calculus, we cannot test variables for
identity. Technically, there is no eq? predicate, like in Scheme.
In order to test for the end of a list, the ‘‘end of list’’ proper ty has to
be encoded in the list itself. The following definitions resemble the
cons, car, cdr, and null? functions of Scheme:

kar ≡ λ p. first (second p)
kdr ≡ λ p. second (second p)
nil ≡ pair T T
nullp ≡ first

element consists of a nested ordered pair (E, (x, y)), where E is a
propositional function indicating the ‘‘end of list’’, x is a list
element, and y is a list containing the other elements. Formally:
(List2)

L = (T , T ) or L = (F, (x, L)) for any list L and value x .

Quotation is a concept that cannot easily be translated to

Inlining Primitive Functions
Inlining a primitive function means that applications of primitive
functions are transformed into special operators instead of
emitting code for an ordinary function application. For instance,
the compiler from the previous section would perform the following
transformation
(car x) → (%apply (%ref 1) x)
while a compiler inlining primitives would translate the same
expression in this way:
(car x) → (%car x)
where %car is a special opcode that will be interpreted as car in
the evaluator.
There are some good reasons to inline primitive functions, but
there are equally valid reasons not to. The obvious drawback is
that when primitive functions are identified by the compiler and
special code is emitted to implement them, they cannot be
redefined later at source level.
E.g., if the compiler would detect car and emit a special opcode
for this primitive function, then any redefinition of the function at
source level would be ignored. For instance, you could not modify
a naive car function to detect non-pairs. However, these problems
arise mostly when bootstrapping a language implementation and
can easily be avoided otherwise.
Advantages of inlining primitive functions include
• more efficient implementation
• oppor tunity for optimization
Inlined code is more efficient, because it saves a function
application at run time.
Fur thermore, the semantics of primitive functions are known from
the language specification, so some information about the
program being compiled can be inferred from their applications.
For instance, we can infer that

62

no matter what the actual values of x and y are. No such
conclusion can be drawn from the code

foo (bar x y)
because the semantics of foo and bar cannot be known at
compile time (unless the whole program would be analyzed, but
even then the semantics of the primitives would have to be
known).
So whether or not to inline primitive functions is largely a matter of

Unrolling the Loops
In the previous iterations of the compiler implementation, a very
abstract vir tual machine has been targeted. A vir tual machine
(VM) is basically a computer system that has been implemented in
software. This approach allows rather complex operations, like
λ -abstraction and application, to be interpreted directly at
‘‘machine’’ level.
The next iteration will target a more low-level architecture, a socalled stack machine. A stack machine is a special case of a
register machine, namely one that arranges its registers in a stack,
or last-in-first-out (LIFO) structure. In a register stack, only the
most recently loaded register(s) can be accessed.
Code generation is easy for a stack machine, because no register
allocation has to be done. The operands to each operation and
function application are always located on the top of the stack. The
conversion from stack machine code to register machine code is
straight-forward and will be explained in the next section. See
[Holm1996] for a formal definition of a stack machine.

The Virtual Machine
The compiler discussed in this section translates Scheme3 to code
for a vir tual machine that will be described semi-formally in the
following.
The virtual machine consists of five indexed sets called the
• instruction store, I
• address store, A
• literal pool, L
• environment, E
• stack, S

and two indexes, the
• stack pointer, P
• continuation, K
The instruction store is a bounded indexed set I = {i 0 , . . . , i k−1 }
that holds instructions to be interpreted by the VM. I n identifies the
instruction in the n th element of the instruction store. The size k of
the set is the number of instructions in the VM program.
The address store is a bounded indexed set A = {a0 , . . . , a k−1 }
that maps subsequent natural numbers n ∈N to arbitrary locations
in the instruction store. It allows to use a number n to identify a
specific part of the program in I , namely I ( An ) . The i in Ai is also
called a label and k is the number of labels in I .
The literal pool L is a bounded indexed set L = {l 0 , . . . , l k−1 }
containing all literal data objects (i.e. quoted symbols and pairs)
occurring in the source program. Literal data objects (literals) will
be referred to as L n instead of inlining them in the VM program.
The reason for this will be explained in the next section. The size k
is the number of literals in the program.
The environment is an unbounded indexed set E = {x 0 , x 1 , . . .}
mapping variables to values or locations. It is an implementation of
→
the environment vector e . E n is the n th value (or location) in the
→
vector (see e notation, pg 54).
The stack is an unbounded indexed set S = {x 0 , x 1 , . . .} and P is
an index to S , such that S P is the first element of S that has an
undefined value, i.e. P = 0 indicates that S is empty.
There are two operations defined on the stack, called push and
pop. The push(x) operation stores x in S P and then increases
the value of P by one. The pop operation decreases P by one and
then returns the value in S P . It is an error to apply pop while
P = 0.

S P−1 is the only argument in single-argument instructions. S P−2 is
the first argument and S P−1 the second argument in two-argument
instructions. I.e. when an instruction is executed, its last argument
is on the top of the stack. See also figure 8, pg 85.

The compiler presented here is a two-stage compiler, where the
first stage is identical to the compiler of the previous section. That
par t of the compiler will be referred to as ‘‘stage 1’’ while the part

instructions of the format described above.
Loop unrolling is the process of replacing a loop with a fixed
number of iterations by a sequence of instructions that mimic
exactly the instructions in the loop. For instance
(for-each push args)
where args has n elements, would be transformed to
(push args1 )
...
(push argsn )
Occasions for loop unrolling would be:
• environment setup in lambda functions
• evaluation of (primitive) function arguments
• sequences of expressions (begin)
• the outer evaluation loop
The latter, though, is replaced by a VM loop for now; it will only be
unrolled when generating native code.

An LC-Based Language

An LC-Based Language
Original LISP and Scheme limit function application to rule (Appm)
(pg 73), i.e. no par tial application is allowed. The number of
arguments to a function must always match the number of
variables of that function. The λ -calculus equivalent of this
statement would be

(AR)

(λ x 1 . . . x n . M)N 1 . . . N m where m = n.

However, even in LISP M can be another abstraction, so there is
an unexplained difference between

(λ x. λ y. M)N
and

(λ xy. M)N
where the first expression yields λ y. M and the second one is an
error. In LISP notation:
((lambda (x) (lambda (y) M)) N) → (lambda (y) M)
but
((lambda (x y) M) N)

Elimination of Boxing
Remember that boxing was introduced for one specific reason and
that was support for mutable variables. So in abstractions that do
not mutate any variables, boxing and unboxing of values can be
omitted.
In a functional language this optimization can probably be applied
to the vast majority of all abstractions. It saves one boxing
operating per function application (when copying the argument to
the environment vector) and one indirection per variable reference.

Elimination of Saved Variables
There are two ways to implement recursive abstractions in
λ -calculus plus mutation. One is the Y -combinator (pg 28) and the
other is the rec schema (pg 71).
When using the rec schema, mutation is used to create a function
that refers to itself, similar to letrec in Scheme. Because one and
the same recursive function F can be applied multiple times in this
case, the binding for the argument of F has to be saved while F
reduces. See pg 71 for an explanation.
This problem does not arise when using the Y -combinator,
because it creates a fresh abstraction for each recursive
application, so the same function can never be applied more than
one time (see also pg 73):

Y ≡ (λ f . (λ x. xx) (λ x. f (λ y. (xx)y)))
ω ≡ ( λ x. F( λ y. (xx)y))

→ F(λ y. (ωω )y)
YF ≡ (ωω ) →
→
→ F(λ y. F(λ y. (ωω )y)y)
→
→ F(λ y. F(λ y. F(λ y. (ωω )y)y)y)
...
Because variable bindings only have to be saved in the presence
of mutation, the save and restore operations for bindings can be
omitted in abstractions that do not use the set construct.
This optimization reduces the size of the stack frame by one,
thereby reducing garbage collection pressure. It does increase the
number of created abstractions, though, so its effect depends the
cost of abstraction.

Saved Variables in Tail-Recursive Abstractions
In a purely tail recursive abstraction, variable bindings do not have
to be saved, even when the rec schema is used.
An abstraction F is tail recursive, when all applications of F in F
appear in tail positions (pg 41). F is purely tail-recursive, when it
does not even recurse through a different abstraction, i.e. there is

Elimination of Saved Variables

141

no abstraction G which is applied by F and return applies F
(unless both of these applications also appear in tail positions).
For instance, the following abstraction is purely tail-recursive,
because the recursion operator r of rec only appears in a tail
position and F does not apply any abstraction that applies F .

F ≡ [rec λ xy. [if nullp x; y; r(kdr x)(S y))]]
Therefore, no variable bindings would have to be saved in the
above term.
The principle of pure tail recursion can theoretically be extended to
cover mutual tail recursion, but this variant would require wholeprogram analysis.

Extension
There are different options for extending a language that is based
on λ -calculus:
• abstractions
• primitive functions
• schematic definitions
Perhaps the most obvious way is to implement additional
functionality by means of λ -abstraction. For instance, Church
numerals and their related functions could be implemented directly
in the language itself.
It might be tempting to add new functions as primitive functions for
several reasons: those functions are often trivial to define in the
implementation language, and for many low-level languages, like
C, there are excellent and mature compilers, which make the
resulting implementation quite efficient.
However, there are also good reasons to keep the core language
small and self-contained: ease of bootstrapping and porting, for
example, as well as maintainability.
Then, there are language elements which cannot be implemented
as abstractions or primitive functions at all, like the rec schema or
the letrec construct of Scheme.

142

Extension

Both of these constructs suffer from similar issues: the rec
schema deliberately ‘‘captures’’ the name of the variable r , i.e., it
uses the name of a specific variable in a context outside of the
scope of the variable:

[rec F] → (λ r. (λ t. (λ xr)[set! r t]) F) (λ xx)
G ≡ λ x. rx
[rec G] ≡ [rec λ x. rx] ≡ (λ r. (λ t. (λ xr)[set! r t]) λ x. rx) (λ xx)
There are two problems here: (1) the term λ x. rx violates the
(Closed) rule (pg 52) and (2) when inserting G into rec, the free
variable r becomes bound, which is ruled out by (S5) (page 11)
and the variable convention (pg 19).
A similar issue arises with letrec, because it attempts to ‘‘inject’’
the names of bound variables into a term which is, again, ruled out
by the variable convention (and by (S3)).
What is needed here is a mechanism that rewrites λ -terms before
reducing them, for instance by inser ting an arbitrary λ -term into
the term of a schematic definition.
In other words, what is needed is a mechanism for ‘‘expanding’’
schematic definitions.

Expanding Schematic Definitions
Several options come to mind for accomplishing this task:
• naive substitution
• rule-based substitution
• metacircular interpretation

For instance, a schemata structure containing a definition of the

Even the if schema could be implemented in this way, given the
proper definitions for T and F (see pg 25).
However, the id construct could not be implemented using a
schematic definition. Can you explain why?
The principle of expanding schematic definitions is, of course, the
same as macro expansion in LISP and schematic definitions can
be thought of as macros.
More sophisticated substitution-based macro expanders use
pattern matching to find a suitable substitution rule for a schema.
To do so, they match not only the name of a schema, like the
above version, but also the structure of the argument or
arguments specified in the application of the schema.
An example for such a system would be syntax-rules templates
as described in [R4RS].
The most flexible approach uses metacircular interpretation to
rewrite schematic definitions. In this case, the term of a schema is
a program that is interpreted at compile time and thereby
generates the expanded schema.
The quasiquote mechanism of Scheme and Common Lisp is
often used in combination with metacircular interpretation to
expand schematic definitions. For instance, the above rec
definition would look like this in quasiquoted notation:
‘((lam r ((lam t (set r t)) ,F)) (lam x x))
Here the entire term is considered to be in NF, except for the F ,
which is explicitly ‘‘unquoted’’ using the comma operator.

Expanding Schematic Definitions

145

Quasiquotation is really only a ver y convenient notation that
expands to combinations of quote, cons, and append. See
[Steele1990] or [R4RS] for details and [Holm2014] for an
implementation in portable Scheme.
Both rule-based and metacircular approaches have their
advantages and drawbacks. For instance, rule-based systems, like
syntax-rules, allow to extend the variable convention to schematic
definitions, while metacircular systems allow for deliberate variable
capturing. Also, the latter provide the full expressiveness of the
source language at macro expansion time. However, name
capturing has to be avoided explicitly using gensym (pg 120) or a
similar mechanism.
In the end it is up to the language designer to decide which
schema expansion system is the best fit for their language.
It is also part of the language designer’s task to create a suitable
syntax for filling the schemata list or a comparable structure with
definitions. The literature provides lots of examples as starting
points. Check out the bibliography for inspiration.
